# Thoughts on LLMs in Programming

I've been programming computers for [most of my life](@/posts/teenage-programmer-alphatronic.md). There's a joy in making
things, and there's a joy in the act of programming itself. Often I gravitate
towards programming for programmers - [frameworks](@/posts/roll-your-own-frameworks.md) for years, [programming
language implementation](@/posts/xee.md) more recently.

Software development has changed incredibly much during my experience with it. I
experienced the era of 8 bit computers with memory counted below the megabyte
and CPUs that ran at a few megahertz to the current always connected
supercomputer in your pocked era. I was there when the internet was new (though
arpanet had a significant history before then). I went from an era where
programming wisdom were tidbits shared with friends, gleaned from books and
magazines, to search, and now AI, at your fingertips.

A lot has remained the same too. Much of the increasing power has been spent
making programming more convenient and easy, rather than making software faster.
[Wirth's Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wirth%27s_law) is with us. LLMs can be seen as the ultimate exponent of this.

But this era of LLM programming is by far the biggest change in software
development that I've seen. You may not be convinced but I am: LLMs writing code
and using tools in a loop, agentic software development, can be astoundingly
effective.

I want to discuss some of my thoughts, my anxieties and my excitement.

## My Response to AI in Programming

My approach to AI has always been driven by curiosity and playfulness. I adopted
AI autocomplete. I was experimenting with vibe coding before it had a name. I've
been using agentic AI intensively for the last half year now. I'm not at the
absolute forefront of any of this, but I'm pretty up to date.

I see people experiment with new approaches, and that's exciting to me.
Stagnation and inertia [frustrate me](@/posts/the-centre-cannot-hold.md). But there are so many experiments here!

## Career Threat

I worry too. Software development is very rapidly changing. Will there be a
place for me?

I have two general answers to this.

### Valuing my Ability to Adapt and Learn

It's plausible that AI is in the end is [normal technology](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/ai-as-normal-technology) and in the long
term ends up creating more jobs than it takes. And so there will be plenty of
room for humans.

I have demonstrated a strong ability to adapt and learn. I am confident I can
adapt to this new world, where I don't write much code by hand, but mold systems
at a different level. It creates a lot of opportunities; I can be more brave
about large refactors and experimentation. Pairing myself with an LLM can make
me tackle and understand things I couldn't do as easily before. 

There are also endless challenges. Complexity remains our greatest enemy. I was
already able to write more code than a team could easily absorb before AI, and
the challenge is on how to organize things to they remain tractable and
maintainable. The ability to read code, and write code, by hand is likely to
going to remain valuable in unexpected ways.

In this world, my knowledge, my ability to understand and learn, and my
creativity will retain its value and I'm going to find my way.

### The Weirder Future

But what if this is truly different?

There's a much weirder future imaginable. LLMs are perhaps just the beginning of
a wave of cognitive technologies. Maybe we can engineer machines that can truly
outclass humans, and do it more cheaply, in any intellectual activity. 

This is not at all a given. As humans we have drives, massive context, human
connection. On the other hand, us humans have been repeatedly shown to be
overconfident about our special place in the universe.

This future is weirder. 

If we can replace any office worker with a machine, we're in for a very wild
ride.

A ridiculous view of this future is that business leaders happily fire most of
their employees, and the status quo continues otherwise. I find such thinking a
massive failure of the imagination. Why would a CEO or billionaire be so unique
in their abilities they cannot be replaced? Or wouldn't it be their ability that
keeps them there, but their power?

This is a recipe for massive, disruptive social change.

This is not a problem for me as an individual that I should be worrying about;
it's a collective concern, like climate change. It requires societal structures
and collective action. Let's talk about this more.

## The positives

Plenty of risks, but I'm not going to dwell on them here. Let's consider some of
the opportunities._[^hand_written] It's a more or less random list.

- Those things I think are super important in software development but
  developers instead often hide behind the codes? Sitting back to think. Talking
  a lot about what we're doing. Looking ahead a little. Creating a shared
  understanding and direction. They become even more important.

- Boring refactors you put off; you can do them far more easily.

- Deep focus taxes mental resources. Letting an AI take over some of the more
  boring reasons this focus is required can give us the space we need to relax,
  be smart, and keep going. (I know that this goes against the grind mindset.
  People instead talk about optimizing workflows by keeping agents running at
  all times, preferably in parallel. I am suspicious of such narratives)

- Experimentation becomes a lot more easy. Experimentation is fun and useful.

- Those frustrating bugs that can take forever to track down? Sometimes an LLM
  has the answer for you much more quickly!

- Non-developers can write significant software. Who knows what they will come
  up with?

- Computer games that embrace generative AI in their core engine; entirely new
  types of games become possible. We've barely scratched the surface of this.

- An understanding of security and edge cases become more important.

- We can create badly structured out of control legacy codebases even more
  quickly than before. Someone is going to have to fix or replace these.

- Understanding and using difficult stuff, like details of low-level programming, or
  graphics programming, or (complex computer science stuff)[] formly out of
  reach becomes more accessible to the AI boosted centaur developer.

- LLM limitations make modularity and structure just as important as they
  already were for humans. Software developers like me have many insights to
  contribute in this area.

- Rewriting code from one language to another becomes a lot easier. This means
  we can port useful libraries faster. 

- We can more easily improve the performance of applications by swapping in a
  more efficient programming language for parts of it.

- I can be more ambitious building [weird artificial life simulations](@/posts/apilar-an-alife-system.md).

- One task that consistently infuriates me whenever I have to do it is
  configuring github actions. This is because of the slow feedback loop - the
  real fix is to make this feedback loop much faster. But now at least I can
  configure an AI agent to do the boring thing for me!

## Conclusion

Do I have no anxieties about this? I certainly do. But I know with mine they're
mixed up with other worries and anxieties I have about life, quite independent
from programming. What helps me manage it is to enjoy life; I grow flowers, I
write a few [poems](https://hyperstate.org), I pursue my many interests and
curiosities, and I hold my family and friends close.
